\section*{Working Papers}

\textbf{\href{https://andreaciccarone.github.io/Video_Partisanship_draft.pdf}{An Image is Worth 1000 Words? Partisanship and Attention in Videos}}

\medskip

\textit{Abstract:} This paper studies how images and text transmit partisanship in video news. I develop a multimodal measure of video partisanship that separates their contributions. Images convey partisanship rapidly through affective cues, whereas text conveys substantive information that accumulates with exposure. An experiment using real news footage shows that these differences shape viewers' responses: partisan images elicit immediate emotional reactions, while partisan text shifts policy attitudes only with sustained exposure. The findings show how low-attention video environments preserve the emotional influence of partisan images while limiting the effects of partisan text on policy attitudes.

\vspace{10pt}

\textbf{\href{https://andreaciccarone.github.io/claim_graphs_draft.pdf}{Mapping Economic Theory with AI}}\\
\textit{with \href{https://elliottash.com}{Elliott Ash} \& \href{https://sergio-galletta.com/}{Sergio Galletta}}

\medskip

\textit{Abstract:} We develop a method for mapping and measuring how economic theory papers reach their conclusions. An agentic AI system represents each paper as a claim graph, which shows how model assumptions and intermediate claims support the reported results. In a random sample of 190 theory papers from four leading economics journals, removing the most consequential starting input leaves an average of 91 percent of weighted headline and major claims unsupported. Next, we benchmark error discovery against published corrections from 100 papers and use claim graphs to assess the consequences of repair. The graphs trace which conclusions depend on mistaken derivations and measure how much of their economic content is lost after correction. While errors in theory papers tend to affect support for a large share of a paper's conclusions, they rarely change the main economic conclusions after the smallest valid repair.

\textbf{\href{https://andreaciccarone.github.io/FETM_draft.pdf}{The Mechanistic Fixed-Effects Topic Model: Recovering Corporate Culture from Employee Reviews}}\\
\textit{with \href{https://dan-biderman.netlify.app/}{Dan Biderman}, \href{https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~blei/}{David Blei}, \href{https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/people/wei-cai}{Wei Cai}, \href{https://www.amirfeder.com/}{Amir Feder} \& \href{https://www.columbia.edu/~ap3116/}{Andrea Prat}}

\medskip

\textit{Abstract:} How can employee reviews reveal comparable dimensions of corporate culture when workers in different industries and review settings describe similar experiences differently? We study this problem using over 900,000 pro and con documents from Glassdoor reviews of S\&P 1500 firms across 20 industries. We introduce the mechanistic Fixed-Effects Topic Model (mFETM), which separates a shared topic component from environment-specific deviations. Topics are distributions over interpretable semantic features extracted from a language model using sparse autoencoders, retaining contextual information beyond word counts. Relative to a model without fixed effects, mFETM improves held-out document perplexity and reduces the association of estimated topic shares with review polarity and industry. Firm-level analyses relate these topics to inclusion in Fortune's 100 Best Companies to Work For and examine how the association changes when environment-associated topics are removed. Finally, we use the estimated fixed effects to steer language generation and assess the resulting changes in review polarity using illustrative generations and held-out likelihood exercises. These results support a framework for measuring shared workplace themes while accounting for systematic differences in how reviews express them.

\vspace{10pt}

\textbf{\href{https://andreaciccarone.github.io/PerformanceTransfers_draft.pdf}{With or Without Conditions? Equity-Efficiency Tradeoffs in Municipal Transfers in Brazil}}\\
\textit{with \href{https://www.luigicaloi.com/}{Luigi Caloi}}

\medskip

\textit{Abstract:} To improve service delivery, central governments can tie intergovernmental transfers to local policy performance. While performance-based transfers incentivize local governments and generate efficiency gains, they also shift transfers from low- to high-capacity governments, creating welfare-reducing inequities. We study this equity-efficiency trade-off using a bundle of transfer reforms to Brazilian municipalities. When two states tied transfers to relative educational performance, student test scores rose substantially: moving from the 25th to the 75th percentile of per capita conditional transfers increased scores by 0.16 standard deviations. However, the reform also widened funding disparities across municipalities, which translated into disparities in municipal expenditures in multiple sectors. In contrast, contemporaneous reforms to unconditional transfers had negligible effects on student outcomes. We use a simple model of optimal transfers to interpret these findings. Our results suggest that the introduction of performance based transfers delivered large efficiency gains, limited welfare losses from inequities, and was welfare-enhancing. We find minimal evidence of multitasking distortions or score manipulation. Instead, we document improvements in the quality of education inputs and find suggestive evidence of reduced corruption.
